Scoring the scan's Gaussian processes as surrogates for Bayesian optimisation

Featurization & kernel scan, run cross_1 · 2,520 configurations · 69 experiments · primary (60 folds, 690 test points) and leave_one_out (60 folds, 69 test points) · 23 cited papers

Background

What the scan ranks today, and why that is not yet the question we ask

We optimise the yield of the NHC-catalysed acyloin condensation by batch Bayesian optimisation (BO). The featurization & kernel scan chooses the Gaussian process (GP) that serves as the BO surrogate, by cross-validating every configuration and ranking it by the gem score.

The gem rewards a GP whose predictive distribution is close to the observed yield on held-out experiments, wherever that yield lies. A BO surrogate is used differently. The acquisition function only acts on the part of the predictive distribution that promises an improvement, so a surrogate is useful when it is right about which conditions give high yield, and harmful when it is confidently wrong there: a confident under-prediction of a good condition keeps the campaign away from it, and a confident over-prediction of a poor condition spends a batch slot on it. This report collects the scoring rules that judge a GP on that basis, tests them on the 69 experiments and on cross_1, and proposes the scores that should rank the scan.

How a model is scored now

CRPS, CRPSS and the gem

For a predictive cumulative distribution function \(F\) and an observed yield \(y\) (as a fraction), the continuous ranked probability score is the squared distance between \(F\) and the step function of the observation, integrated over every yield level \(\theta\):

\[ \mathrm{CRPS}(F, y) = \int_{-\infty}^{\infty} \big(F(\theta) - \mathbb{1}\{y \le \theta\}\big)^2 \, d\theta = \mathbb{E}_F|X - y| - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_F|X - X'| \]

It is in yield units and reduces to the absolute error for a point forecast. The skill score compares the model with the baseline forecast \(\mathcal{N}(\bar y_{\text{train}}, s^2_{\text{train}})\), the training mean and variance quoted for every test point, pooled over every test point of every fold of a study:

\[ \mathrm{CRPSS} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i} \mathrm{CRPS}_i(\text{model})}{\sum_{i} \mathrm{CRPS}_i(\text{baseline})}, \qquad \text{gem} = \min\big(\mathrm{CRPSS}_{\text{primary}},\ \mathrm{CRPSS}_{\text{leave-one-out}}\big) \]

CRPSS = 0 means the model is worth nothing over quoting the training average; the gem is the worse of the two interpolation studies.

Terms used throughout

Proper. A scoring rule \(S\) is proper if the expected score under the true distribution \(G\) is minimised by quoting \(G\) itself, \(\mathbb{E}_G S(G, Y) \le \mathbb{E}_G S(F, Y)\) for every \(F\); strictly proper if \(G\) is the only minimiser. A proper score cannot be improved by quoting something other than one's best belief, so it ranks honest forecasts above hedged ones.

Threshold decomposition. The integrand of the CRPS at level \(\theta\) is the Brier score of the probability \(1 - F(\theta)\) that the yield exceeds \(\theta\). The CRPS is therefore the sum, over every yield level, of how well the model forecasts "this experiment beats \(\theta\)".

Studies. primary holds out whole catalysts, 6 folds repeated 10 times, so every experiment is a test point 10 times (690 pooled test points); leave_one_out holds out one replicate group at a time (60 folds, 69 test points).

Part I

CRPS, CRPSS and MAE price an error by its size, not by where it falls

Seven synthetic predictions on the yield-fraction scale, each a Gaussian \(\mathcal{N}(\mu, \sigma^2)\) scored against one observed yield. They differ in where the observation lies, in the direction of the miss and in the predictive spread. All numbers are computed in closed form.

1. The same miss costs the same at 5 percent and at 70 percent yield

For a BO surrogate the seven cases are not equally harmful. Case A hides the best condition behind a confident prediction of 40 percent; case B promises 35 percent for a condition that gives 5 percent, which costs one batch slot and is corrected by the next measurement. One would expect a score for BO surrogates to rank A far worse than B.

The mechanism is the threshold decomposition. A miss from 0.40 to 0.70 makes the forecast of "yield beats \(\theta\)" wrong for every \(\theta\) between the two values, and the CRPS adds those Brier errors with weight 1 per unit of yield. An interval of length 0.30 costs the same wherever it lies on the yield axis and in whichever direction the miss points. The CRPS does reward an honest spread: the wide prediction C costs 0.1870 against 0.2831 for the overconfident A, a factor of 1.51. MAE and RMSE do not see the spread at all.

CRPS already leans on the high yields, for the wrong reason

On the 69 experiments the 11 yields above 0.40 are 16 percent of the pooled test points but carry 38 to 39 percent of the baseline's summed CRPS in both studies, because they lie far from the training mean of about 0.18. The emphasis comes from the size of the errors a constant forecast makes there, not from their value to the optimisation, and it applies equally to a far-off low yield.

Part II

Weighting by the observed yield rewards the optimist: the forecaster's dilemma

The direct repair, scoring high-yield test points more heavily or only scoring the test points whose observed yield is high, makes the score improper. We show the theorem, a simulation and the effect on a real cross_1 configuration.

2. A weight on the observation changes which forecast is optimal

Let \(S_0\) be a proper score and \(w \ge 0\) a weight function of the observed yield, and score with \(S(F, y) = w(y)\, S_0(F, y)\). If the yield has true density \(g\), the expected score is minimised not by \(g\) but by the reweighted density (Gneiting and Ranjan 2011, Theorem 1):

\[ \mathbb{E}_g\big[w(Y)\, S_0(F, Y)\big] \text{ is minimised by } f^{\ast}(y) = \frac{w(y)\, g(y)}{\int w(z)\, g(z)\, dz} \]

With \(w(y) = \mathbb{1}\{y \gt t\}\), the case of scoring only the high-yield test points, the optimal quote is the true distribution conditioned on exceeding \(t\): a model that always predicts high yield. Restricting the evaluation to extreme outcomes "discredits skillful forecasts" and favours forecasters who always predict the extreme (Lerch, Thorarinsdottir, Ravazzolo and Gneiting 2017). For a BO surrogate this is the optimist the campaign must not choose, since it spends every batch on conditions it wrongly believes to be good.

3. The optimist wins the restricted score in simulation and on real folds

In a simulation shaped like the campaign, the latent mean yield follows \(0.9 \cdot \mathrm{Beta}(0.9, 3.5)\) and the observation adds Gaussian noise with standard deviation 0.08, for 200,000 cases of which 12.4 percent exceed \(t = 0.40\). The ideal forecaster quotes the true conditional distribution. The hedged forecaster raises every mean below 0.45 to 0.45, which is the strategy the theorem recommends for the restricted score. The rule that follows

Emphasis on high yields has to enter through the forecast, never through the observation: a weight on the yield levels \(\theta\) at which the forecast is judged, a weight on the forecast's quantile levels, or a censoring of the forecast. Each of these keeps the score proper (Part III). The weight may depend on anything known before the test yield is revealed, including the training data of the fold, which is what makes a data-driven weight admissible (Part V).

Part III

Five proper ways to focus a score on a region, and what each costs

The literature offers five constructions that emphasise part of the outcome range and stay proper, plus calibration diagnostics for the same purpose. Each is stated with its definition, its propriety, what it sees of location and spread, how it is computed, and how it fits the scan's outcome transforms and small test folds.

4. Threshold weighting judges the forecast at the yield levels we care about

Threshold-weighted CRPS

A non-negative weight \(w(\theta)\) on the yield levels replaces the uniform weight of the CRPS (Matheson and Winkler 1976; Gneiting and Ranjan 2011). With a chaining function \(v\), any antiderivative of \(w\), it is the CRPS after mapping forecast and observation through \(v\) (Allen, Ginsbourger and Ziegel 2023):

\[ \mathrm{twCRPS}(F, y; w) = \int w(\theta)\big(F(\theta) - \mathbb{1}\{y \le \theta\}\big)^2 d\theta = \mathbb{E}_F|v(X) - v(y)| - \tfrac{1}{2}\,\mathbb{E}_F|v(X) - v(X')|, \qquad v'(\theta) = w(\theta) \]

The weight sits on the levels at which the forecast is judged, not on the observation, so the score is proper for every weight.

Propriety. Proper for every \(w \ge 0\) (Matheson and Winkler 1976). Strictly proper when \(v\) is injective, that is when \(w \gt 0\) almost everywhere (Allen, Ginsbourger and Ziegel 2023). With a one-sided indicator \(w(\theta) = \mathbb{1}\{\theta \gt t\}\) the score only depends on the forecast above \(t\) and is strictly locally proper there (Holzmann and Klar 2017, Theorem 4); it then equals the CRPS of the forecast censored at \(t\) against \(\max(y, t)\), the censored CRPS of de Punder, Diks, Laeven and van Dijk (2026). Censoring at the incumbent \(t = y^{\ast}\) gives the CRPS of the improvement \(\max(Y - y^{\ast}, 0)\), the random variable whose expectation is expected improvement.

Location and spread. It is a CRPS on the chained scale, so it penalises a wrong centre and a wrong spread at the weighted levels, and it penalises a miss in either direction by the weight of the yield interval between prediction and observation. In Part I, case A costs 0.2796 and case B 0.0000 under \(w = \mathbb{1}\{\theta \gt 0.40\}\), and the false optimum F still costs 0.2331, because the yields between 0.40 and 0.65 that it wrongly promised are weighted levels.

Computation. Closed form for a Gaussian forecast and an indicator weight (Friedli, Gautier, Broccard and Ginsbourger 2025, Theorem 1), the sample form above for draws (Allen 2024), and one-dimensional quadrature of the exact predictive CDF otherwise. Fit with the scan. It is defined on the raw yield scale, so every outcome transform is scored on the same axis once its exact raw-scale CDF is available (Part VI). Its noise is set by how few test points fall where \(w\) is large.

5. Quantile weighting sets the direction, not the region

The CRPS is also the integral of quantile scores over the quantile level \(\alpha\), and weighting those levels gives the quantile-weighted CRPS (Gneiting and Ranjan 2011):

\[ \mathrm{qwCRPS}(F, y; u) = \int_0^1 u(\alpha)\, 2\big(\mathbb{1}\{y \le F^{-1}(\alpha)\} - \alpha\big)\big(F^{-1}(\alpha) - y\big)\, d\alpha \]

It is proper for every \(u \ge 0\). The quantile score at level \(\alpha\) charges an under-prediction \(2\alpha\) and an over-prediction \(2(1 - \alpha)\) per unit of yield, so a weight on high \(\alpha\) makes missing a high outcome dearer than promising one. It emphasises the forecast's own upper quantiles rather than a region of the yield axis, so on its own it does not single out high yields. Combined with threshold weighting it gives exactly the asymmetry BO needs (Part V).

6. Outcome weighting and the likelihood scores need a correction and carry their own costs

Outcome-weighted CRPS. Holzmann and Klar (2017) restore propriety to an observation weight by scoring the forecast's own reweighted distribution \(F_w\) instead of \(F\), \(\mathrm{owCRPS}(F, y) = w(y)\, \mathrm{CRPS}(F_w, y)\). It is only proportionally locally proper: it judges the shape of the forecast in the region and is blind to the probability the forecast assigns to the region. Adding a Brier score for that probability makes it strictly locally proper, but sums a squared probability with a quantity in yield units; in Part I this rates the false optimum F at 1.00 and the missed optimum A at 0.52, a ratio fixed by the units, not by the problem. It also requires \(\mathbb{E}_F[w(X)] \gt 0\), which fails for sharp forecasts away from the region, and Allen, Bhend, Martius and Ziegel (2023) recommend the threshold-weighted form for that reason.

Conditional and censored likelihood. Diks, Panchenko and van Dijk (2011) score the log density inside the region and, for the censored version, the log probability of falling outside it. Both are proper, and the censored likelihood ratio test is uniformly most powerful for comparing two forecasts on the region (de Punder, Diks, Laeven and van Dijk 2026). They inherit the log score's unbounded penalty: case A costs 47.4 under the censored likelihood against 0.28 under the threshold-weighted CRPS. This is the fold-level heavy tail that makes the scan quote MSLL as a median, and a pooled sum of such scores is set by its worst point.

Weighted kernel scores. The vertically re-scaled CRPS and the threshold-weighted energy and variogram scores extend the construction to several outcomes at once (Allen, Ginsbourger and Ziegel 2023). For a single outcome and an indicator weight with its canonical anchor the re-scaled CRPS equals the threshold-weighted CRPS, so they add nothing for yield alone. They are the route to a joint yield and selectivity score.

7. Two limits that no weighting removes

A weighted score can only judge the region with the data that falls into it. Lerch et al. (2017) find that tests of equal performance based on weighted scores gain little or no power over tests based on the unweighted log score, and that any advantage vanishes as the weighted region becomes rarer. Brehmer and Strokorb (2019) prove that genuine tail properties, defined in the limit of extreme levels, cannot be elicited by any expected score. Here the relevant region is not an asymptotic tail but the upper third of the observed yields, so the second result does not bind; the first does, and Part V measures it.

For point forecasts the same idea exists as a mixture of elementary scores over decision thresholds (Ehm, Gneiting, Jordan and Krüger 2016; Taggart 2022); a region-weighted absolute error is the median-forecast special case and loses the spread. Conditional PIT histograms and tail-calibration diagnostics (Allen et al. 2023; Allen, Koh, Segers and Ziegel 2025) check calibration given an exceedance, paired with a reliability check of the exceedance probability. They condition on the outcome and are therefore diagnostics, never ranking scores.

Part IV

Global accuracy and calibration do not reliably predict BO performance; accuracy near the optimum does

Six studies relate a surrogate's test-set scores to the outcome of the optimisation it drives, on synthetic functions, hyperparameter tuning, molecules and proteins. None is on reaction conditions, and all optimise single points rather than batches.

8. Within a model family, calibration error does not track regret

The expectation behind calibrated BO is that a surrogate whose 80 percent intervals hold 80 percent of the outcomes makes better exploration decisions (Kuleshov, Fenner and Ermon 2018). Deshpande, Marx and Kuleshov (2024) argue it from the acquisition side: probability of improvement and expected improvement are expectations under the predictive distribution, which a calibrated model estimates correctly on average, and online recalibration improved their benchmarks. Foldager, Jordahn, Hansen and Andersen (2023) measured the relation across 128 synthetic problems and 6 tuning tasks. Calibration error and total regret correlate at 0.28 and 0.42 over all runs, but the partial correlation given the surrogate type is -0.06 and -0.24: the association comes from GPs and deep ensembles being both better calibrated and better optimisers, not from calibration within a family. Recalibration did not lower regret, and inflating every predictive standard deviation by a factor above 1 raised regret faster than shrinking it.

The chemistry studies agree. In DIONYSUS, "good prediction and calibration of a surrogate model on a test set does not necessarily correspond to better Bayesian optimization" (Tom, Hickman, Zinzuwadia, Mohajeri, Sanchez-Lengeling and Aspuru-Guzik 2023). On protein landscapes, uncertainty-quality metrics were not well correlated with gains between active-learning rounds, and including the uncertainty in the acquisition did not necessarily improve on greedy selection (Greenman, Amini and Yang 2025).

9. Ranking and local accuracy near the optimum do track it

Tom, Lo, Corapi, Aspuru-Guzik and Sanchez-Lengeling (2025) correlated the surrogate's test performance with the fraction of the top 100 molecules found. The \(R^2\) of the regression fit gave no correlation with \(p \lt 0.05\); Kendall's rank correlation on the test set correlated at 0.44 to 0.92 across their dataset groups and models, the GP at 0.54 to 0.91. Li, Cui, Mansi, Prakash and Liao (2026) split the test log-likelihood by the label: over their configurations top-k recall correlates at Spearman -0.31 and -0.22 with the global log-likelihood and at 0.31 to 0.65 with the log-likelihood on the test points in the top 1 to 10 percent.

What predicts a good optimisation is how well the surrogate orders and predicts the candidates near the top, not its average accuracy or average calibration.

The last result is the forecaster's dilemma in practice: restricting the likelihood to the true top points is an observation weight and could be gamed by an optimistic model if used to choose one (Part II). It shows which region matters, and threshold weighting is the proper way to score that region. Friedli et al. (2025) use the threshold-weighted CRPS to focus GP-driven sequential design on molecules above a property threshold, and Petit, Bect, Feliot and Vazquez (2023), comparing likelihood and scoring-rule criteria for selecting GP parameters, find that "the choice of an appropriate family of models is often more important than the choice of a particular selection criterion", the same pattern as the model-type effect of Foldager et al.

How far the evidence carries

The correlations come from single-point BO on large candidate pools, with tens to hundreds of iterations. Our campaign has 69 experiments, batches of six, and a mixed continuous and categorical space. The direction of the evidence is consistent across studies; its size is not transferable.

Part V

For this campaign the weight must follow the data, and a missed optimum must cost a bounded multiple of a false hope

We first measure what a fixed high-yield threshold does on the 69 experiments, then build a weight from each fold's own training yields and an asymmetric binary score from the cost-loss representation of proper scores. Every number in this Part is computed from the rebuilt predictive distributions of cross_1 (Part VI) or from the synthetic cases of Part I.

10. A fixed threshold puts the score on four catalysts

Only 11 of the 69 yields exceed 0.40 and 4 exceed 0.50. The 11 come from four catalysts and include five replicates (three at 0.43 to 0.44, two at 0.50 and 0.51), so they are eight distinct conditions. The incumbent is useless as a threshold for evaluation: the best training yield of a fold is 0.67 in 50 of 60 primary folds and 59 of 60 leave_one_out folds, and only 10 of 690 and 1 of 69 test points exceed it. We scored 150 configurations of cross_1 (the 40 best by gem and 110 drawn at random) with the indicator weight \(\mathbb{1}\{\theta \gt 0.40\}\) and with a smooth weight \(\Phi((\theta - 0.35)/0.10)\).

11. The training yields themselves define the weight

For a fold with training yields \(y_1, \dots, y_n\) we weight the yield level \(\theta\) by the fraction of training experiments it beats, the empirical distribution function of the training yields:

\[ w_n(\theta) = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \mathbb{1}\{y_i \le \theta\}, \qquad v_n(\theta) = \int_{-\infty}^{\theta} w_n(u)\, du = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \max(\theta - y_i, 0) \]

The chaining function \(v_n\) is the average improvement of a yield \(\theta\) over the experiments already run. Scoring on that scale generalises the improvement over the incumbent, \(\max(\theta - y^{\ast}, 0)\), which uses only the best experiment and is too sparse to evaluate (item 10), to the improvement over every past experiment. On the 69 experiments the weight is 0.19 at a yield of 0, 0.33 at 0.05, 0.62 at 0.20, 0.84 at 0.40, 0.97 at 0.60 and 1 from 0.67 up, so an error interval at the top of the range costs three to five times the same interval at the bottom. There is no threshold to choose: the weight rises with the campaign's own results and moves upward as better yields are found. It is fixed by the training data before the test yield is seen, so the score stays proper, and it is positive above the lowest training yield (0 in every fold), so \(v_n\) is strictly increasing there and the score is strictly locally proper on the whole yield range.

12. A cost-loss mixture makes a missed optimum k times dearer than a false hope

The threshold decomposition judges, at every level \(\theta\), the forecast probability \(p = 1 - F(\theta)\) that the experiment beats \(\theta\). For BO this is a go or no-go decision at every level. Running a condition that does not beat \(\theta\) wastes a batch slot; not running one that does loses the improvement. Every regular proper score for a probability is a mixture of elementary cost-loss scores (Ehm, Gneiting, Jordan and Krüger 2016): with cost-loss ratio \(c\), acting when the event fails costs \(c\) and not acting when it occurs costs \(1 - c\), and the forecaster acts when \(p \gt c\). The Brier score, and with it the CRPS, mixes the ratios uniformly. A mixing density \(k(1 - c)^{k - 1}\), which favours small \(c\), gives the proper binary score

\[ s_k(p, 1) = \frac{2k}{k + 1}(1 - p)^{k + 1}, \qquad s_k(p, 0) = \frac{2}{k + 1}\Big(1 - (1 - p)^{k}(1 + k p)\Big) \]

for the event occurring (\(1\)) or failing (\(0\)). For \(k = 1\) it is the Brier score. A confident miss, \(s_k(0, 1) = 2k/(k + 1)\), costs exactly \(k\) times a confident false alarm, \(s_k(1, 0) = 2/(k + 1)\), so \(k\) is the price of a lost optimum in units of a wasted experiment, and it is bounded by construction. Because each elementary score is proper, so is every mixture, for every \(k\).

BO-weighted CRPS

Integrating the asymmetric binary score over the yield levels with the training-yield weight gives

\[ \mathrm{BOCRPS}_k(F, y) = \int w_n(\theta)\, s_k\big(1 - F(\theta),\ \mathbb{1}\{y \gt \theta\}\big)\, d\theta \]

which has the equivalent quantile form, by Theorem 1a of Ehm et al. (2016),

\[ \mathrm{BOCRPS}_k(F, y) = \int_0^1 k\,\alpha^{k-1}\; 2\big(\mathbb{1}\{y \lt F^{-1}(\alpha)\} - \alpha\big)\big(v_n(F^{-1}(\alpha)) - v_n(y)\big)\, d\alpha \]

It is the threshold-weighted CRPS on the scale of average improvement over past experiments, with the upper quantile levels weighted by \(k\alpha^{k-1}\); for \(k = 1\) it is the threshold-weighted CRPS with weight \(w_n\), and with \(w_n \equiv 1\) and \(k = 1\) it is the CRPS.

Part VI

The metrics of choice: a BO-weighted skill score that ranks, and its threshold decomposition that explains

Two metrics, one for ranking and one for diagnosis, both computed from the exact predictive distribution of every fold. The pending decision is the asymmetry \(k\) and whether the BO gem replaces the gem as the ranking criterion.

15. Metric 1: the BO-weighted CRPS skill score and the BO gem

Definition

Per test point \(i\) of a fold with training yields \(y_1, \dots, y_n\), the model's predictive CDF \(F_i\) and the baseline \(B_i = \mathcal{N}(\bar y_{\text{train}}, s^2_{\text{train}})\) are scored with the same weight \(w_n\) and asymmetry \(k\) (item 12). Per study the skill is pooled over every test point of every successful fold, and the BO gem is the worse of the two interpolation studies:

\[ \mathrm{BOCRPSS}_k = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i} \mathrm{BOCRPS}_k(F_i, y_i)}{\sum_{i} \mathrm{BOCRPS}_k(B_i, y_i)}, \qquad \text{BO gem} = \min\big(\mathrm{BOCRPSS}_{k,\text{primary}},\ \mathrm{BOCRPSS}_{k,\text{leave-one-out}}\big) \]

BOCRPSS = 0 means the model is worth nothing over the training average for deciding which conditions beat the campaign's past results; a missed improvement costs \(k\) times a wasted experiment.

Why this score. It is the only candidate that meets all three requirements at once. It scores the whole predictive distribution and penalises a wrong centre and a wrong spread (item 4). It weights the high-yield region through the forecast and a weight fixed by the training data, so it is proper and does not reward the optimist (items 2, 3 and 13), and the weight follows the campaign without a hand-set threshold (item 11). Its asymmetry is a single number with a decision meaning, the price of a missed improvement in wasted experiments, bounded by construction (item 12). On cross_1 it carries half of its mass on the 11 high-yield test points at 1.15 times the noise of CRPSS (item 14).

Choice of \(k\). We propose \(k = 2\): a confident miss of a condition that would have improved on the campaign costs two wasted experiments. In the synthetic cases this makes the missed optimum 2.8 times as costly as the same miss at low yield and 2.1 times as costly as an honest wide prediction, against 1.0 and 1.5 under CRPS; on cross_1 its bootstrap noise is lower than at \(k = 1\) (0.076 against 0.086), and \(k = 3\) and \(k = 4\) change the leaders further with the same noise (item 14). The choice of \(k\) is a statement about the campaign's costs, not something the data can estimate.

Skill score and pooling. The pooled skill is a ratio of sums over the same test points, exactly as for CRPSS. A skill score is in general not proper, because the reference score depends on the observations (Gneiting and Raftery 2007). Here every configuration is scored on the same folds against the same baseline, so the denominator is a common constant and the ranking by \(\mathrm{BOCRPSS}_k\) is the ranking by the summed score itself, which is proper. The baseline is scored with the same \(w_n\) and \(k\), so 0 keeps its meaning. Failed folds are dropped and flagged as for the gem.

16. Metric 2: the threshold decomposition locates the loss on the yield axis

The BO-weighted score is an integral over yield levels, so the same pooled skill can be restricted to a band of levels: the share of the score lost or gained when deciding whether an experiment beats a yield in that band. The weight \(w_n\) is nearly constant within a narrow band and cancels between model and baseline, so the band skill measures the forecast of the exceedance probabilities alone. It is a diagnostic for a chosen configuration, not a ranking score: each band rests on few test points. For the best cell of each outcome transform, at \(k = 2\):

17. Computing it on cross_1 and on later runs

Every fold of cross_1 stores the fitted raw parameters and the training indices. Building the cell's model on those indices with the current code and loading the parameters reproduces the fitted GP without refitting; the predictive distribution of an observation is then Gaussian on the model's outcome scale, \(Z \sim \mathcal{N}(m, s^2)\) with \(s^2\) the posterior plus noise variance, and the raw-scale CDF is exact: \(F(\theta) = \Phi\big((g(\theta) - m)/s\big)\) for the monotone probit and raw links \(g\), and a sum over the periodic branches of \(\sin^2\) for arcsin_sqrt. The scan stores only the moments of 2,000 draws for the non-raw transforms, so this rebuild is what makes a weighted score exact for them.

All 2,520 configurations, 137 folds each with no failed fold, were rebuilt and passed the check against what the run stored before their predictions were kept. For raw cells the rebuilt predictive mean and variance must equal the stored ones to \(10^{-6}\); the largest deviation of the min-over-studies CRPSS recomputed from them is \(9.4 \times 10^{-10}\). For the other transforms the exact raw-scale mean and variance (Gauss-Hermite quadrature) must differ from the stored 2,000-draw moments by Monte Carlo error only: the root-mean-square z-score of both, over all test points of a cell, must stay below 1.5. On the best cells it is 0.96 to 1.02, as Monte Carlo error predicts, and shifting the rebuilt mean by 2 percent of a predictive standard deviation while widening the spread by 2 percent already raises it to 1.5; a model built differently from the fitted one cannot pass. The rebuild costs about 3 CPU-seconds per configuration and the scoring about 12 CPU-seconds for eight score variants.

For later runs the same quantities are computed inside the fold: the fit already holds \(m\) and \(s\) for every test point, so the score is added to the fold record next to the CRPS, with no rebuild.

18. How the BO gem enters the ranking

We propose that the BO gem at \(k = 2\) replaces the CRPSS gem as the criterion that ranks the scan and chooses the production surrogate, with the CRPSS gem kept as a reported column. Both are proper; they differ in what they reward, and only the BO gem rewards what the acquisition function uses. Configurations whose BO gems differ by less than the bootstrap noise, about 0.08 on the leading configurations, are ties, and the choice among them is made on other grounds, as for the gem today. The threshold decomposition is reported for the configurations that reach the top of the ranking.

The other studies (type, scaffold, solvent, replicate) are scored with the same BO-weighted skill and reported without entering the BO gem, as now. The full cross_1 table with both gems and the decomposition of the leaders is the next step once \(k\) and the role of the BO gem are fixed.

What the BO gem cannot show

It judges one-point predictive distributions on held-out experiments. It does not see the joint distribution across a batch, which qLogNEI uses, nor the acquisition optimiser. The direct test is a retrospective BO run of the leading configurations, which the literature in Part IV recommends and which no single-point score replaces.
