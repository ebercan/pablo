Kernel structures and prior mean functions for the NHC acyloin Gaussian process

Elimination run of 26.09.2026 · 69 experiments · 50 catalyst levels · 576 of 600 configurations · gem pooled over primary and leave_one_out · 46 papers · kernel_mean_1 sweep (96 configurations, 6 anchors)

Background

Which covariance structure and which prior mean the surrogate should use

We optimise the yield of an NHC-catalysed acyloin condensation by batch Bayesian optimisation over catalyst, solvent, base and three continuous conditions. The surrogate is a single-task Gaussian process on yield, and its accuracy on experiments it has not seen sets how well the optimiser ranks the 161 candidate catalysts.

The elimination run swept four kernel structures and four mean functions, each in a one-axis sweep around one baseline, on all 50 catalyst levels. The crossed run cross_1 that followed holds the kernel structure at KS1 and the mean at constant, because the alternatives were indistinguishable from them in the elimination run and the simpler choice was kept. This report diagnoses the two axes in detail, derives what a better structure or mean has to do, reviews the literature against those requirements and proposes three kernel structures and three mean functions with the sweep that tests them.

The structures and means compared

Every structure is built from four block kernels, one per feature block: catalyst (cat), solvent (solv), base (base) and continuous conditions (cont: formaldehyde ratio, catalyst loading, temperature, time). Each block kernel \(k_b\) is a unit-variance Matérn-5/2 or RBF kernel with its own lengthscales, under a log-normal prior located at \(\log\sqrt{D} + \sqrt{2}\), and every lengthscale satisfies \(\ell \ge 0.05\). The 29 base-free experiments and experiment 27 carry an all-zero base block and the flag base_active \(= 0\).

How a model is scored

Pooled CRPSS and the gem

The continuous ranked probability score (CRPS) compares a predictive distribution with the observed yield; lower is better. The skill score pools it over every held-out experiment of every successful fold of a study and compares it with the trivial forecast \(\mathcal{N}(\bar{y}_{\text{train}}, s^2_{\text{train}})\) of the same fold:

\[ \mathrm{CRPSS} = 1 - \frac{\sum_{i} \mathrm{CRPS}_i(\text{model})}{\sum_{i} \mathrm{CRPS}_i(\text{baseline})} \]

CRPSS = 0 means the model is worth nothing over quoting the training average; the gem is the smaller of the pooled CRPSS on primary and on leave_one_out. primary holds out whole catalysts (60 folds, 51 training and 18 test experiments), so it measures transfer to an unseen catalyst. leave_one_out holds out one experiment (60 folds, 68 and 1), so it measures interpolation among catalysts that are in the training set.

Noise floor and verdicts

Bootstrap noise. Folds of primary and leave_one_out are resampled with replacement, 2,000 draws, paired across the configurations compared. The median bootstrap standard deviation of one configuration's gem is 0.074 for KS1 with the constant mean and 0.083 to 0.126 for the alternatives. A level is resolved when the 95% interval of its paired gem difference excludes zero.

Verdicts. An alternative lost when it is worse on most levels with a paired test below 0.05; it tied when its across-level bootstrap interval contains zero and most levels are unresolved; it won when the interval excludes zero.

The mechanism is the product. In KS1 the covariance between a held-out catalyst and the training rows is \(\sigma^2 k_{\mathrm{cat}} k_{\mathrm{rest}}\); a catalyst far from the training catalysts in descriptor space has \(k_{\mathrm{cat}} \approx 0\), and its prediction reverts to the training mean whatever the solvent, base and temperature. An additive term in solv, base or cont carries the condition response to that catalyst. On leave-one-out the held-out experiment's catalyst is in the training set, the full product describes its specific response, and spreading weight over additive terms costs accuracy.

Limitation

Every number in this Part is at the elimination baseline. At the cross_1 anchors the solvent and base blocks are low-dimensional descriptors, the outcome is probit-transformed and the prior is P2; six of the seven per-level best cross_1 configurations bind on primary, where the additive structures help, so the ties here do not carry over without a run.

Cost is not only the parameter count

The timing probe puts block_linear at 14.8 s per fold against 4.5 s for the constant mean at 44 columns, where it has about 48 coefficients, and at 129 s against 12.6 s at 5,028 columns. The width-dependent part comes from the catalyst slopes; the 3.3-fold cost at 44 columns is paid by a mean of a few dozen coefficients fitted jointly with the kernel by L-BFGS.

Part IV

The literature, by family, against these requirements

46 papers were read in full text. Each is cited by DOI or arXiv identifier and by its file in archived_trash/kernel_mean_research/. Every formula used in Part V was checked against the paper.

11. Mixtures of product and additive terms

The additive GP of Duvenaud, Nickisch and Rasmussen weights each interaction order \(n\) by its own variance \(\sigma_n^2\) (their Eq. 3), so main effects and the full product coexist with independent variances rather than on a simplex. CoCaBO (Ru et al., Eq. 2) and CASMOPOLITAN (Wan et al., Eq. 4) reduce this to one trade-off parameter fitted by marginal likelihood:

\[ k = (1-\lambda)(k_h + k_x) + \lambda\, k_h k_x \]

HyBO (Deshwal et al.) puts a horseshoe prior on the order weights so unused orders shrink to zero. The orthogonal additive kernel (Lu, Boukouvalas and Hensman) constrains each component to integrate to zero under the input measure (their Eq. 6 and 8; for a categorical input Appendix C, Eq. 20), which makes main effects and interactions identifiable.

Against it: the papers fit on hundreds of points or more (Duvenaud et al.: 167 to 8,192 points; Lu et al.: 208 and up), the mixture parameter is often weakly identified, and none treats a block that is absent on some rows. The orthogonality constraint needs an input measure, which for the catalyst block is the 161-catalyst library.

12. Hierarchical and categorical structure

Hensman, Lawrence and Rattray model replicates and clusters as a shared parent process plus deviations, which on the stacked data is \(k_g + \delta\, k_f\): every member inherits the shared covariance, and only members of the same group share the deviation. lgpr (Timonen et al.) makes such deviations identifiable with the zero-sum kernel, 1 for equal categories and \(-1/(M-1)\) otherwise (their Eq. 1), and shrinks component scales with half-Student-t priors whose mode is zero. Group and nested kernels (Roustant et al.; Carpintero Perez et al.) parameterise categorical correlation through group structure with a handful of parameters and beat free-form categorical kernels in a 42-dataset benchmark.

The hierarchical form fits the diagnosis directly: a condition response shared by all catalysts, plus a catalyst-specific deviation scaled by catalyst similarity. Against the categorical kernels: about 25 catalysts appear in a training fold and 161 are candidates, so any kernel indexed by catalyst identity (latent coordinates, free-form or group covariances) has no information about the catalysts held out in primary. They are useful for the four pre-catalyst forms, not for the catalyst itself.

13. Inputs that are absent on some rows

For the base block on base-free rows the literature offers three treatments. Swersky et al. embed an inactive input at a fixed point of a cylindrical (arc) embedding; Zaefferer and Horn show that imputing a fixed reference value keeps the kernel positive semidefinite and gives inactive rows the same prior variance; Jenatton et al. build tree-structured covariances in which shared parents are linear terms integrated out analytically. KS1 already uses the imputation form; KS2 to KS4 use a mask, which none of these papers recommends without renormalisation.

14. Prior mean functions

O'Hagan and Rasmussen and Williams (Section 2.7) give the Gaussian process with explicit basis functions: with \(g(x) = f(x) + h(x)^{\top}\beta\) and \(\beta \sim \mathcal{N}(b, B)\), integrating \(\beta\) out gives (their Eq. 2.40)

\[ g \sim \mathcal{GP}\big(h(x)^{\top} b,\; k(x,x') + h(x)^{\top} B\, h(x')\big) \]

The coefficients disappear from the optimisation, and the predictive variance carries their uncertainty, growing with \(\lVert h(x) \rVert\) away from the data. Kennedy and O'Hagan use the same device for a mechanistic model plus a discrepancy process, with weak priors on the regression coefficients integrated out. De Ath, Fieldsend and Everson find, over ten synthetic and three real problems, that linear and quadratic means fitted by ridge regression do not lower BO regret and often raise it, while a pessimistic constant helps in five or more dimensions. Ziatdinov, Ghosh and Kalinin fit parametric physical means jointly with the kernel by NUTS and find them decisive when the shape is right.

15. Selection, shrinkage and marginalisation at small n

Piironen and Vehtari calibrate shrinkage priors by the expected number of non-zero effects and warn against point estimates of the global scale. SAAS (Eriksson and Jankowiak) switches dimensions off with a half-Cauchy prior on inverse lengthscales. Lotfi et al. show that the marginal likelihood can prefer models that generalise worse on small data and propose the conditional marginal likelihood; Bachoc shows that leave-one-out estimation of covariance parameters is more robust than maximum likelihood when the covariance family is misspecified. Simpson, Lalchand and Rasmussen and Lalchand and Rasmussen find that marginalising hyperparameters on small datasets improves predictive calibration over a point estimate.

For 51 training points the literature favours a few interpretable components with shrinkage priors and cross-validated comparison over a search across many structures. Kernel search by marginal likelihood is the approach Lotfi et al. and Bachoc warn about at this size, and it costs tens to thousands of fits per decision. Full marginalisation is the principled fix for weakly identified mixture parameters and is the natural second stage for whichever proposal is accepted.

Part V

Three kernel structures, three mean functions and the sweep that tests them

Every proposal contains KS1 with the constant mean as a special case, so a fit that finds no use for the new component returns to the current model. All new priors are proper and have closed-form densities, so each proposal can be sampled by NUTS.

17. KS5: product with a catalyst correlation floor

\[ k(x,x') = \sigma^2 \big[\rho + (1-\rho)\, k_{\mathrm{cat}}(x,x')\big]\, k_{\mathrm{solv}}(x,x')\, k_{\mathrm{base}}(x,x')\, k_{\mathrm{cont}}(x,x'), \qquad \rho \sim \mathrm{Beta}(1, 3) \]

Two experiments run under the same conditions correlate at least \(\rho\) whatever their catalysts. This is the hierarchical form of Section 12 with the shared and deviation processes using the same condition kernel: \(\sigma^2\rho\, k_{\mathrm{rest}}\) is the response shared by all catalysts, \(\sigma^2(1-\rho) k_{\mathrm{cat}} k_{\mathrm{rest}}\) the catalyst-specific part. It addresses Section 2 directly: a held-out catalyst with \(k_{\mathrm{cat}} \approx 0\) keeps covariance \(\sigma^2\rho\, k_{\mathrm{rest}}\) with training rows at similar conditions. Beta(1, 3) has its mode at \(\rho = 0\) (which is KS1) and mean 0.25, with finite density at zero, so the MAP fit can return to KS1. One extra hyperparameter; measured at 1.31 to 1.42 times the cost of KS1; base-free rows keep the KS1 zero location (RK4). It is one method on KernelStructure that wraps the catalyst block kernel. The risk is identification: with long catalyst lengthscales \(k_{\mathrm{cat}} \approx 1\) everywhere and \(\rho\) is flat, so its fold-to-fold spread is part of the acceptance test.

18. KS6: product plus shrunk condition main effects

\[ k(x,x') = \sigma_{\times}^2\, k_{\mathrm{cat}}\, k_{\mathrm{solv}}\, k_{\mathrm{base}}\, k_{\mathrm{cont}} + \sigma_{+}^2\, \tfrac{1}{3}\big(k_{\mathrm{solv}} + k_{\mathrm{base}} + k_{\mathrm{cont}}\big), \qquad \sigma_{\times}^2 \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(3, 4),\; \sigma_{+} \sim \mathcal{N}^{+}(0, 0.5^2) \]

This is the Duvenaud additive kernel restricted to order 1 and order 4, with two changes the diagnosis asks for: the catalyst has no main effect (a large \(w_{\mathrm{cat}}\) costs leave-one-out, Section 4), and the main effects carry their own scale with a half-normal prior whose mode is zero instead of sharing a Dirichlet simplex with the product (RK2, RK5). The main effects reuse the block kernels and lengthscales of the product, so the only extra hyperparameter is \(\sigma_{+}\); the prior mean of \(\sigma_{+}^2\) is 0.25 against 0.75 for the product. The base main effect is unmasked and uses the zero location, so base-free rows keep their variance. Cost: measured at 1.03 to 1.37 times KS1. It is a KernelStructure method using _block_kernel(block, mask=False).

19. KS7: shared condition process plus a library-centred catalyst deviation

\[ k(x,x') = \big[\sigma_g^2 + \sigma_d^2\, \tilde{k}_{\mathrm{cat}}(x,x')\big]\, k_{\mathrm{solv}}\, k_{\mathrm{base}}\, k_{\mathrm{cont}}, \qquad \tilde{k}_{\mathrm{cat}}(c,c') = k_{\mathrm{cat}}(c,c') - \frac{m(c)\, m(c')}{\bar{M}} \]
\[ m(c) = \frac{1}{L}\sum_{l=1}^{L} k_{\mathrm{cat}}(c, c_l), \qquad \bar{M} = \frac{1}{L^2}\sum_{l=1}^{L}\sum_{l'=1}^{L} k_{\mathrm{cat}}(c_l, c_{l'}), \qquad L = 161 \]

The catalyst deviation is constrained to average to zero over the 161 library catalysts, the orthogonality of Lu et al. (Appendix C, Eq. 20, with uniform weights over the library) applied to the catalyst block. The shared term \(\sigma_g^2 k_{\mathrm{rest}}\) is then the library-average response and the deviation cannot absorb it, which removes the trade-off that makes \(\rho\) in KS5 weakly identified. \(\tilde{k}_{\mathrm{cat}}\) is a conditional covariance and stays positive semidefinite. Priors: \(\sigma_d^2 \sim \mathrm{Gamma}(3,4)\) as for the KS1 outputscale, \(\sigma_g \sim \mathcal{N}^{+}(0, 0.5^2)\). One extra hyperparameter. Its extra cost is one \(n \times 161\) and one \(161 \times 161\) catalyst Gram matrix per evaluation, which grows with catalyst width: measured at 1.5, 3.6 and 4.3 times KS1 on the grover_pca (29 columns), scage_carbene (about 530) and unimol (1,559) anchors. The library catalyst rows are carried by CellFeatures.cat_library and normalised with the fold's training ranges, the map the model's input transform applies. KS7 is implemented and tested but is not part of the sweep in Section 24, where its width-dependent cost would set the run's duration.

20. M1 (cond_linear): condition-linear mean

\[ m(x) = c + a_{\mathrm{solv}}^{\top} x_{\mathrm{solv}} + a_{\mathrm{cont}}^{\top} x_{\mathrm{cont}} + \beta(x)\big(b_{\mathrm{base}} + a_{\mathrm{base}}^{\top} x_{\mathrm{base}}\big), \qquad a_b \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I/d_b),\; b_{\mathrm{base}}, c \sim \mathcal{N}(0, 1) \]

Here \(\beta(x)\) is base_active. This is block_linear without the catalyst slopes, the identified part of Section 7. Its parameter count is \(d_{\mathrm{solv}} + d_{\mathrm{base}} + d_{\mathrm{cont}} + 2\), about 10 to 30 at the anchors and independent of catalyst width (RM1, RM2). Extrapolation is linear (RM3) and shrinkage comes only from the fixed priors. Cost: measured at 2.46 to 2.75 times the constant mean on three anchors, without a trend in catalyst width. It is the _MEAN_BUILDERS key cond_linear.

21. M2 (cond_trend): integrated condition-linear trend

\[ m(x) = c, \qquad k_{\mathrm{M2}}(x,x') = k(x,x') + s^2\, h(x)^{\top} D\, h(x'), \qquad h(x) = \big[x_{\mathrm{solv}},\, \beta(x),\, \beta(x)\, x_{\mathrm{base}},\, x_{\mathrm{cont}}\big], \qquad s \sim \mathcal{N}^{+}(0, 1) \]

The coefficients of M1 are given the prior \(\mathcal{N}(0, s^2 D)\), with \(D\) holding \(1/d_b\) for each block and 1 for the base intercept, and integrated out (Rasmussen and Williams, Eq. 2.40; O'Hagan, Theorem 2). The trend costs one hyperparameter \(s\) instead of 11 to 30 coefficients; \(s \to 0\) returns the constant mean (RM4), and the predictive variance carries the slope uncertainty, so the trend raises uncertainty rather than confidence away from the data. Cost: one low-rank kernel term, measured at 0.89 to 1.10 times the constant mean. It is the mean key cond_trend: build_mean_module returns the constant mean and build_mean_kernel_term the trend term, which the model factory adds to the kernel.

22. M3 (form_offset): partially pooled pre-catalyst form offsets

\[ m(x) = c, \qquad k_{\mathrm{M3}}(x,x') = k(x,x') + \tau^2\, \mathbf{1}\big[t(x) = t(x')\big], \qquad \tau \sim \mathcal{N}^{+}(0, 0.5^2) \]

Each pre-catalyst form \(t\) (salt, free NHC, \(\mathrm{CO_2}\) adduct, bicarbonate) gets an offset \(b_t \sim \mathcal{N}(0, \tau^2)\), integrated out, so the four offsets are partially pooled toward the constant and their spread is learned. The per-form intercepts are the identified part of type_linear (median |z| 0.397, 90 percent sign stability). Integrating the offsets rather than fitting them avoids the degenerate MAP of a hierarchical variance (\(\tau \to 0\) with all \(b_t = 0\)). It is the random-effect version of the zero-sum categorical term of lgpr and is predictable for every library catalyst, since the form is known for all 161. Cost: one rank-4 term, measured at about 1.0 times the constant mean. It is the mean key form_offset, built as M2 is. The form determines base_active, so M3 also carries the base versus no-base offset.

Why these and not the other families

Categorical kernels on catalyst identity cannot predict a held-out catalyst. Physical temperature means rest on about 22 experiments off 80 °C. Kernel search and averaging over many structures cost tens of fits per decision and select by a marginal likelihood that misleads at 51 points. Purely additive structures repeat KS2. Each proposal adds one interpretable component with one scale whose prior includes zero, which is what the small-data papers of Section 15 support.

24. The sweep that tests them

The proposals are tested where the scan now operates, on the best cross_1 configuration of six of its seven catalyst levels, rather than at the elimination baseline; unimol, whose 1,559-column design makes it the slowest anchor, and KS7 are not in the sweep. Each anchor is crossed with four kernel structures (KS1, KS2, KS5, KS6) and four means (constant, cond_linear, cond_trend, form_offset), with every other axis at the anchor's level and all 137 folds of the current cv_splits.json. KS1 with the constant mean refits each anchor in the same run as the paired control, which also removes the selection effect of picking the best of 2,520 configurations and refits the probit anchors under the per-scale noise prior.

Acceptance and rejection

A proposal is accepted when, against the anchor refit in the same run, its paired fold-bootstrap gem difference is resolved positive on at least four of the six anchors and resolved negative on none. Its leave-one-out pooled CRPSS must not be resolved worse on any anchor, it must have no failed fold, and its cost must stay within 1.5 times the anchor for the kernels and 3.5 times for M1.

The rule is stated on the pooled gem. The BO gem of bo_surrogate_metrics.html (its Metric 1, the BO-weighted CRPS skill score) is computed for every configuration and reported next to it; an anchor on which the two disagree in sign counts as unresolved.

The new scale parameter must be identified: across the 60 primary folds its standard deviation is below its prior standard deviation and its median lies away from zero. A proposal whose scale sits at zero has returned to KS1 or the constant mean and is reported as such.

A proposal that is indistinguishable from the anchor on all six anchors is rejected, and KS1 with the constant mean stays. When two proposals are both accepted and indistinguishable from each other, the one with fewer extra hyperparameters and lower cost is kept. The type, scaffold, solvent and replicate studies are reported for every configuration, and a mean that lowers the leave-one-type-out CRPSS on more than half the anchors is flagged for M3 in particular.

Limitation

Six anchors give little resolving power: with a per-configuration gem standard deviation of 0.074 to 0.126, only a gain of roughly 0.1 resolves on a single anchor. The sweep can show that a proposal helps at the current best configurations; it cannot rank the proposals across all 50 levels. A positive result is followed by fully Bayesian fits of the accepted proposal on the anchors, since every new prior is proper.

26. The condition-linear mean and KS2 are resolved worse

The expectation from Section 7 was that dropping the catalyst slopes leaves a mean whose coefficients are identified. The measurement is that fitting those coefficients as point estimates still costs more than it gives. cond_linear is resolved worse on transformerm_precat_pca with each of KS1, KS5 and KS6 (-0.100 to -0.104), on both the gem and leave-one-out, and loses 0.20 to 0.23 of gem on scage_carbene, with intervals reaching +0.04 to +0.06. Its leave-one-type-out pooled CRPSS falls at all six anchors, by 0.09 to 1.05, so the fitted slopes extrapolate badly to an unseen pre-catalyst form.

KS2 is resolved worse on minimol_precat_pca with every mean (-0.087 to -0.174, leave-one-out -0.15 to -0.26) and, with cond_linear, on grover_pca as well (-0.288).

This repeats Section 2 at the operating point: the additive structure gives up interpolation among measured catalysts. Seven proposals fail the leave-one-out condition of the rule on one anchor each: the three cond_linear cells on transformerm_precat_pca and the four KS2 cells on minimol_precat_pca. The other eight are resolved worse on no anchor, on either the gem or leave-one-out.
