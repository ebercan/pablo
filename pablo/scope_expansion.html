Scope expansion: deciding which locked catalyst to order

pablo/scope_expansion.py · batches of 6 experiments over the available catalysts · 1 catalyst ordered per iteration · dead time of 4 batches · fantasy tree of depth 4 with (12, 4, 2, 1) children per node · operating modes only_terminal, averaged, layer_averaged

Background

The ordering decision

We optimise the yield of the NHC-catalysed acyloin condensation by batch Bayesian optimisation. Every iteration a Gaussian process is fitted to all measurements and six new experiments are chosen with qLogEI. An experiment can only use a catalyst that is on the shelf, and most of the 161 azolium catalysts in the library are not: they have to be bought or synthesised first. Every iteration we may order one of these locked catalysts, and it arrives several batches later.

Scope expansion is the ordering decision. It is a different question from choosing the batch. The batch is run now, with the catalysts we have and the model we have. An ordered catalyst is run later, with the catalysts we will have then and the model we will have then. The module answers it with a lookahead that simulates the batches the campaign runs while the catalyst is in transit, and scores each locked catalyst by its value at the end of that simulation.

1. Three sets of catalysts and a dead time

At every iteration each catalyst of the library is in exactly one of three sets. The functions receive them as tensors of discrete rows, one row per catalyst, solvent and base combination, and split each set into its unique catalyst, solvent and base tables before optimising over it. A catalyst that needs no external base carries a zero base block, as everywhere else in pablo.

2. The current model values a catalyst as if it could be used now

The obvious criterion is the one the batch generator already uses: the qLogEI of the current model, restricted to experiments whose catalyst is locked. An experiment \(x\) is a full point of the search space: temperature, formaldehyde ratio, catalyst loading, time (pinned at 20 h), and the catalyst, solvent and base blocks. The catalyst of the best \(x\) is ordered. This is optimize_scope_expansion_myopic.

Myopic criterion
\[ \alpha_{\text{myopic}}(x) = \operatorname{qLogEI}_0(x) = \log \mathbb{E}_0\big[\max\big(f(x) - f^{*}_0,\, 0\big)\big] \]

\(\mathbb{E}_0\) is the expectation under the posterior of the current model and \(f^{*}_0\) is the best measured yield. It is the expected improvement of an experiment run in the next batch, which a locked catalyst cannot be.

One would expect this to be a sound rule, since it orders the catalyst with the most promising experiment. Its weakness is timing. Before the ordered catalyst is used, four batches of six, 24 experiments, are chosen from the catalysts on the shelf, and they change both quantities the expected improvement depends on. The posterior variance falls wherever those experiments land, and the incumbent \(f^{*}\) rises whenever they succeed. A locked catalyst whose descriptors sit close to an available one has a high expected improvement today, but the campaign explores that region with the available neighbour long before the order arrives.

The quantity that matters is the expected improvement of a locked catalyst under the model that will exist when it arrives, and that model is not known today.

Part I

Fantasising the batches run while the catalyst is in transit

We do not know what the next four batches will measure, but the Gaussian process gives a distribution over it. A fantasy replaces an unknown measurement by a sample from that distribution and treats it as if it had been measured. Repeated over several samples and several batches, it gives a set of plausible future models, and each locked catalyst is scored under all of them. The tree is built by build_fantasy_tree.

3. One fantasy step simulates a batch, samples its yields and conditions on them

A fantasy step takes a node model \(M\) and performs three operations. First, it chooses the batch the campaign itself would choose from that state: qLogEI of \(M\) against its own incumbent, optimised by optimize_acqf_mixed_pool_greedy with the same (2, 2, 2) temperature partition and pinned time as the real batch, over the catalysts usable at that depth. Second, it draws \(F\) joint samples of the noise-free yields at the six batch points from the posterior of \(M\), using a Sobol quasi-random normal sampler. Third, it conditions \(M\) on each sample as if it had been measured, which gives \(F\) child models.

\[ X_M = \arg\max_{X} \operatorname{qLogEI}_M(X), \qquad \tilde{y}^{(b)} \sim p_M\big(f(X_M)\big), \qquad M_b = M \mid \big(X_M, \tilde{y}^{(b)}\big), \quad b = 1, \dots, F \]

The maximum runs over batches of six experiments whose catalysts lie in the allowed set of that depth, and is found approximately by the pool-greedy optimiser. Conditioning uses condition_on_observations: the child is the exact posterior with the six fantasised points appended to the training data, under the parent's hyperparameters and noise variance. Nothing is refitted, so every model in the tree is the root model conditioned on the fantasised batches along its path. This keeps the tree cheap (Part IV), and it is also its main limitation: a simulated future cannot change the lengthscales, which a real refit after 24 new experiments can.

Two properties of the child drive everything that follows. Its posterior variance is lower around the six new points. Its incumbent \(f^{*}_{M_b}\) is the largest of the measured and the fantasised yields (best_observed_f reads the child's training targets), so a branch in which the simulated batch did well sets a higher bar for every later improvement.

4. Repeating the step builds a tree of plausible futures

The step is applied depth by depth. Every node of layer \(\ell - 1\) gets its own simulated batch and as many children as num_fantasies gives for that depth, so layer \(\ell\) holds models conditioned on \(\ell\) simulated batches. Different branches simulate different batches, because each node optimises its batch on its own posterior. A child's batch optimisation receives its parent's batch as a warm start, so the parent's choice is always among its candidates.

5. Each depth sees the catalysts usable at that time

The simulated batch at depth \(\ell\) is optimised over the catalysts the campaign will actually have then: the available set plus every order that arrives by batch \(t+\ell\).

\[ \mathcal{A}_\ell = \mathcal{A}_{\text{avail}} \cup \bigcup_{j=1}^{\ell} \mathcal{O}_j, \qquad \ell = 1, \dots, k \]

Here \(\mathcal{O}_j\) is x_ordered[j-1], the orders that become usable in batch \(t+j\). An order placed two iterations ago therefore enters the simulated batches from its arrival on, and x_ordered may hold at most \(k\) entries. The catalyst being chosen now is in none of the \(\mathcal{A}_\ell\). The simulated future is the one in which the campaign runs without the candidate, with every catalyst already on order, which is exactly what makes the criterion count redundancy.

6. Fantasies lower the value of catalysts the campaign would cover anyway

Under a deep node the expected improvement of a locked candidate \(x\) is computed against a posterior that has seen up to 24 more experiments and an incumbent that may have risen. If the simulated batches tested experiments similar to \(x\), its posterior variance has fallen and the fantasised yields there are already part of the incumbent, so its improvement shrinks. If they did not, its variance is unchanged and only the incumbent moved. The ranking of locked catalysts under the tree therefore differs from the myopic ranking in a systematic direction.

What the tree measures

A locked catalyst keeps a high score under the fantasised models only if it promises improvement in a region the simulated batches did not reach, or above the incumbents they raised. Scope expansion with fantasies orders the catalyst that adds what the shelf cannot provide in the next four batches, not the catalyst that looks best today.

Part II

The three operating modes aggregate the tree differently

The tree yields 252 models. The expansion criterion of a candidate experiment is a weighted mean of its qLogEI under them, and the operating mode sets the weights. fantasized_criterion builds it; OPERATING_MODES lists the three modes. The myopic criterion is the same construction with all weight on the root.

7. The criterion is a weighted sum of per-node qLogEI

Terminal criterion
\[ \alpha(x) = \sum_{\ell=1}^{k} \sum_{\nu \in \mathcal{L}_\ell} w_\nu \operatorname{qLogEI}_\nu(x), \qquad \operatorname{qLogEI}_\nu(x) = \log \mathbb{E}_\nu\big[\max\big(f(x) - f^{*}_\nu,\, 0\big)\big] \]

\(\mathcal{L}_\ell\) is layer \(\ell\) of the tree, \(\mathbb{E}_\nu\) the expectation under node \(\nu\), \(f^{*}_\nu\) the node's incumbent including its fantasised yields, and the weights \(w_\nu\) sum to 1. Each member qLogEI uses 128 quasi-Monte Carlo samples. The root is not a member in any of the three modes. Every node judges the candidate against its own future: its own posterior and its own incumbent.

The criterion is a WeightedSumAcquisition over the nodes with a positive weight. The weights come from _aggregation_weights, which lists the nodes layer by layer. Because the average is taken over log values, \(\alpha\) is the logarithm of a weighted geometric mean of the per-node expected improvements. A catalyst whose expected improvement is near zero in a fraction of the futures is penalised more than it would be under an arithmetic mean, so the criterion prefers catalysts that are useful in most futures over catalysts that are very useful in a few.

8. only_terminal scores the catalyst at its arrival

\[ w_\nu = \begin{cases} 1 / |\mathcal{L}_k| & \nu \in \mathcal{L}_k \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} \]

Only the deepest layer counts, uniformly. A layer-\(k\) model has seen the \(k\) simulated batches, so it is a fantasy of the model that chooses batch \(t+k+1\), the first batch in which the ordered catalyst can be used. With \(k = D\) this mode matches the timing of the decision exactly. It also rests entirely on the deepest fantasies: each of its 96 models carries 24 fantasised yields, sampled in sequence under fixed hyperparameters, and all of them share the 12 sampled outcomes of the first simulated batch.

9. averaged weights every node equally

\[ w_\nu = \frac{1}{\sum_{\ell=1}^{k} |\mathcal{L}_\ell|} \quad \text{for every node } \nu \]

Every model of layers 1 to \(k\) gets the same weight, 1/252 in run_5. A layer's share is therefore its share of the nodes, and with branching (12, 4, 2, 1) the two deepest layers hold 192 of the 252 nodes. averaged puts 76.2 percent of its weight on layers 3 and 4 and 4.8 percent on layer 1, so it sits close to only_terminal.

10. layer_averaged weights every layer equally

\[ w_\nu = \frac{1}{k \, |\mathcal{L}_\ell|} \quad \text{for } \nu \in \mathcal{L}_\ell \]

Each layer contributes its mean, and the criterion is the mean over layers, so each of the four layers carries 25 percent regardless of its size. A layer-1 node, which rests on one simulated batch, weighs eight times as much as a layer-4 node. layer_averaged is the default of optimize_scope_expansion_fantasized and of the fantasised strategy in run_5.

What the shallower layers add

Scoring a catalyst under a layer-1 model scores an experiment that cannot happen at that time, so the shallower layers are never more accurate about timing than layer \(k\). What they offer is a smaller stack of assumptions: a layer-1 model rests on one simulated batch, a layer-4 model on four, each chosen by an approximate optimiser and conditioned without a refit. only_terminal takes the timing exactly and the fantasy error fully; averaged and layer_averaged accept a timing error to dilute the fantasy error. Which trade pays off is what the run_5 sweep measures, by scoring every pick under every criterion.

Part III

From criterion to order

optimize_scope_expansion_fantasized builds the allowed set of every depth, builds the tree, turns it into the criterion of the chosen mode, and then runs a two-stage terminal step. Only the first stage decides what is ordered.

12. Stage 1 picks the expansion experiment among the locked catalysts

The expansion_optimizer maximises the terminal criterion over q_expand experiments whose catalysts come from x_unordered, with \(0 \lt q_{\text{expand}} \lt q_{\text{batch}}\). The temperature grouping applies only in part: _expansion_batch_constraints passes the partial partition for q_expand points, which is no constraint at all for up to three points under (2, 2, 2) and (2, 1, 1) for four. The result is a full experiment, catalyst, solvent, base and conditions; the catalyst block is the order, and the conditions are where the criterion places that catalyst, not a commitment for the experiment run after arrival. With more than one expansion point, every node's qLogEI values the points jointly, so two near-identical catalysts score little more than one.

13. Stage 2 completes a batch and reports its value

The completion_optimizer builds a full batch of q_batch experiments over the terminal allowed set, the available set plus every entry of x_ordered, with the expansion points fixed at the head of the batch (pre_batch). The function returns the criterion value of that completed batch next to the expansion points. Stage 2 does not influence which catalyst is ordered; it reports the terminal value of a batch that uses the new catalyst alongside everything else on the shelf.

14. run_5 builds one tree per iteration and cross-scores every pick

The run_5 benchmark calls the two building blocks directly instead of optimize_scope_expansion_fantasized. After every real batch it builds one fantasy tree on the refitted model with build_fantasy_tree, derives all three criteria from it with fantasized_criterion, and adds the myopic qLogEI of the root. Each of the four criteria picks one locked experiment (stage 1 only, q_expand = 1), and a random expander draws a locked catalyst uniformly and places it at its best point under the myopic criterion. All five picks are scored under all four criteria and stored as expansion_scores; the operating mode of the job decides which pick is ordered. The ordered catalyst becomes usable at batch \(t+D+1\), and the next trees see it in their allowed sets from that depth on. The pools of the real batch and of the five picks are seeded by the landscape seed, the benchmark and the iteration, not by the mode, so the five modes of one seed draw the same pools until their orders differ.

Part IV

Why a 252-model tree is affordable

Building the tree takes 157 batch optimisations, and scoring a candidate pool under the criterion takes up to 252 posteriors per candidate. Both are cheap because every node differs from the root only by a few appended rows, and the optimiser is built to exploit that.

15. Every node reuses the root's factorisation

GPPoolPosteriorCache holds the Cholesky factor of the root's training covariance, the whitened residuals, and, for every candidate pool \(P\) it has seen, the whitened cross-covariance and the root posterior on \(P\):

\[ L_0 L_0^{\top} = K(X_0, X_0) + \sigma_n^2 I, \qquad \beta_0 = L_0^{-1}\big(y_0 - m(X_0)\big), \qquad W_0 = L_0^{-1} K(X_0, P) \]

A node with fantasised inputs \(X_f\) and targets \(\tilde{y}_f\) is the root with \(f\) extra rows and the same hyperparameters, so its posterior on \(P\) follows from a block Cholesky update of size \(f\) alone:

\[ \begin{aligned} W_f &= L_0^{-1} K(X_0, X_f), \qquad L_f L_f^{\top} = K(X_f, X_f) - W_f^{\top} W_f + \sigma_n^2 I, \\ \beta_f &= L_f^{-1}\big(\tilde{y}_f - m(X_f) - W_f^{\top}\beta_0\big), \qquad V = L_f^{-1}\big(K(X_f, P) - W_f^{\top} W_0\big), \\ \mu_\nu(P) &= \mu_0(P) + V^{\top}\beta_f, \qquad \sigma_\nu^2(P) = \sigma_0^2(P) - \operatorname{diag}\big(V^{\top} V\big) \end{aligned} \]

In run_5, \(f\) is at most 24, against 69 or more root rows. The cache verifies before reuse that a model's training inputs begin with the root's and that its hyperparameters are identical; every model built by condition_on_observations from the root passes by construction. The expensive factor \(W_0\) of a pool is computed once and shared by every node that scores that pool.

16. Shared pools, warm starts and member-wise scoring

Every batch optimisation at depth \(j\) draws its random pool rows with seed \(j\) from the same component tables, so all nodes of a depth, up to 96 calls, score the same random rows and reuse one \(W_0\). Their warm-start rows, the rows around their own incumbents and their refined rows differ. The expansion stage uses seed \(k\) and the same cache, so every member of the criterion shares the root factors of the expansion pool as well. The pool-greedy optimiser scores a WeightedSumAcquisition member by member on its fast path. Each member's value is the larger of its Monte Carlo qLogEI and the best single-point analytic log EI in the batch, a lower bound on log qEI that stays informative where no QMC sample reaches the improvement region. The optimiser itself is described in pablo/pool_greedy_optimizer.html.

17. A tree model holds its GP caches only while it is used

The pool evaluator needs only the root cache and each node's \(f\) extra rows. GPyTorch's own prediction caches of a node, about 22 dense matrices of the node's training size, are needed only for the node's batch, its fantasy samples and its children. build_fantasy_tree therefore releases them on every non-root node once its children exist, and the run_5 criteria are built with release_member_caches=True, so a forward call (the final value of a pick, the cross-scores) rebuilds and drops one member's caches at a time. At 663 training rows a 28-model tree peaks at 1.5 GB instead of 4.9 GB; the rebuilds add about 3 s per pick and per cross-scoring pass.
