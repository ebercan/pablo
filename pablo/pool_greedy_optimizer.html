The pool-greedy batch optimizer and the fantasy tree in run_5

run_5 scope extension · q = 6 batches · temperature partition (2, 2, 2) · time pinned at 20 h · fantasy tree [12, 4, 2, 1] · 157 batch optimisations per fantasized expander call

Background

Every BO iteration in run_5 has to answer two questions. The first is which six experiments to run next on the catalysts that are already in the lab. The second is which catalyst that is not yet in the lab to order now, knowing it arrives four iterations later. Both questions end up as the same numerical problem: choose a set of candidate points that maximises qLogEI on some GP. The first question needs this once per iteration. The second question, in its fantasized form, needs it 157 times per iteration, once for every node of a tree of simulated futures. The optimizer that solves this problem is optimize_acqf_mixed_pool_greedy. It has to be fast enough to be called 157 times in about a minute and good enough that the batches it returns are worth running.

A candidate point is one row of the search space. It holds four continuous values (temperature, formaldehyde ratio, catalyst loading and time, with time fixed at 20 h), and the descriptor vectors of one catalyst, one solvent and one base. A batch is six such rows. The three temperatures used in a batch must form the partition (2, 2, 2): three temperature levels, two rows at each.

What the optimizer maximises

For a batch B of six rows, qEI(B) is the expected amount by which the best of the six outcomes beats the current best yield best_f:

qEI(B) = E[ max(0, maxi Yi − best_f) ],  with Y1 … Y6 drawn jointly from the GP posterior

The optimizer works with its logarithm, qLogEI, because late in a run the values become tiny (EI around e−20) and only the log keeps them comparable. Because the six outcomes are drawn jointly, qEI rewards batches whose members are not redundant: two rows the GP considers almost identical succeed or fail together, and the second adds almost nothing. This is the property the greedy construction exploits.

Part I · The fantasy tree

1. Why a tree of simulated futures is needed

A catalyst ordered now can first be used four iterations from now. Whether it was a good order depends on what the model will believe at that time, and that depends on the 24 experiments run in between, whose yields are not known yet. The fantasized expander handles this by simulating those four iterations. A simulated experiment result is called a fantasy: a yield drawn from the GP posterior at a point that has not been measured. Conditioning the GP on fantasy yields gives a fantasy model, which is the GP as it would look if those yields had been observed. The kernel hyperparameters are not refitted; only the training data grows.

One simulated iteration works like this. Take a model (at the start, the real GP). Optimise a q = 6 batch on it, using the catalysts that are available at that point in the simulated future. Draw m joint samples of the six yields at that batch, where m is the number of fantasies for that depth. Condition the model on each sample. The result is m child models, one per possible outcome of that batch. Repeating this from every child builds a tree. With fantasies [12, 4, 2, 1], the root gets 12 children, each of those gets 4, each of those 2, and each of those 1.

Depth-4 nodes need no batch of their own, since nothing is simulated beyond them. That is why the 253 nodes cost 157 batch optimisations. The catalysts a batch may use grow with depth: a node at depth j may use the catalysts available today plus the ones ordered in earlier iterations that have arrived by that point.

2. How the tree picks the catalyst to order

Once the tree is built, the expander runs two more optimisations on it. Stage 1 picks the expansion point: one row whose catalyst is still locked (not yet in the lab and not yet ordered). Its score is a weighted average of that row's log EI over the fantasy models, so a catalyst is preferred when it looks promising across many plausible futures, not just under today's model. Averaging logs makes this a geometric mean of EI: a row that is hopeless in many futures is penalised hard even if it is excellent in a few. The weights are set by operating_mode. The expander runs after the current batch has been measured and the GP refitted, so the tree starts from the newest real data.

Stage 1 is a q = 1 problem, so the greedy construction of Part II reduces to scoring every pool row once and keeping the best. The catalyst of that row is the one ordered. Stage 2 completes a full batch around the chosen row and reports its value in selected_source_a_acq_values. It does not influence which catalyst is ordered.

Why the tree calls are cheap

Every node in the tree is the real GP plus at most 24 fantasy points, with the same hyperparameters. The expensive part of a GP posterior over a pool of candidates is solving against the n training points, which is O(n²) per candidate with n up to 652. That solve is done once for the real GP and cached. A node only adds a correction for its own f ≤ 24 fantasy points, which costs O(f) per candidate instead of O(n). Nodes at the same depth share one random pool, so the cached part is computed once per depth rather than once per node.

Part II · One batch, step by step

The optimizer never searches over all possible six-row batches. It first builds a finite table of single candidate rows, the pool, and then chooses the batch from that table. The Sobol sequence fills only the continuous columns of most of those rows. The word greedy describes how rows are taken from the table.

3. Step 1: build the pool

The pool is built from four sources. Each source adds a fixed number of complete rows, and the pool is simply their concatenation. Nothing is deduplicated and nothing is removed.

The Sobol sequence is only 3-dimensional. It spreads the 4,096 random rows evenly over temperature, formaldehyde ratio and loading. Time is written into every row as 20 h. Catalyst, solvent and base of a random row are drawn independently and uniformly, and the base slice is set to zero when the catalyst needs no external base. The 256 local rows are 32 copies of each of the 8 training points with the highest targets, fantasy points included, each copy jittered. A fair coin decides whether a copy keeps its incumbent's catalyst, solvent and base or gets random ones. The refined rows are added next to the rows they were refined from, so the pool holds both versions.

The other roles use the same recipe with different counts: stage 1 of the expander has 4,096 + 256 + 8 = 4,360 rows, and stage 2 has 512 random rows only.

4. Step 2: refine the most promising single rows

Every row of the first three sources is scored on its own, with the same screening estimate the greedy step uses, bounded below by its analytic LogEI. The 32 best rows then have their continuous values tuned by 30 iterations of L-BFGS-B on analytic LogEI, with catalyst, solvent and base held fixed. The tuned copies are appended to the pool. The high-EI region is narrow once the model has located a good area, and a random row seldom lands in it exactly.

5. Step 3: greedy construction

The batch is built one slot at a time. For slot 1 every pool row is scored on its own and the best is kept. For slot 2 every remaining row is scored as a pair with slot 1, using qLogEI, and the row that makes the best pair is kept. Slot 3 is chosen the same way against slots 1 and 2, and so on up to slot 6. Each choice is final. That is what greedy means here: each slot takes the best available addition given what is already chosen, and no earlier choice is revisited. Within a slot the search is exhaustive, since every pool row is scored.

Scoring 4,384 rows six times with 1,024 QMC samples each would be slow. Each step therefore screens first. All rows are ranked on 32 QMC samples with a cheap approximation, and only the best 64 are scored exactly on all samples. The random rows and the other rows are screened as two separate blocks, so 128 rows get the exact score. Exhaustive search over every six-row subset of the pool would need about 1019 evaluations. The greedy construction needs about 6 × 4,384 ≈ 26,000 cheap scores plus the joining candidates below.

Temperature grouping is handled inside the same loop. A group is a set of rows that share one temperature, and the batch needs three groups of two. At each step a candidate row can either open a new group, keeping its own temperature, or join a group that already has one row, in which case its temperature is overwritten with the group's value. Joining is tried on the 256 rows that ranked best in that step's screen, once per joinable group. All options are scored and the best single row across them is taken. A move is only allowed if the partition can still be completed: a fourth group cannot be opened, and a group that already has two rows cannot be joined. The finished batch therefore always has exactly (2, 2, 2). Section 9 shows the real sequence of opens and joins.

6. Steps 4 and 5: swaps and polish

In the main batch role, one swap pass follows. Each of the six slots in turn is removed and chosen again from the pool with the other five fixed, and the new row is kept if the batch value rises. Tree nodes skip this. Last comes the polish: L-BFGS-B on qLogEI over the continuous values of all six rows at once, with catalysts, solvents, bases and grouping held fixed. Each temperature group has a single temperature variable, so the two rows of a group move together and the partition survives. The polished batch replaces the greedy one only if its value is higher.

The batch value V, and why it has a lower bound

Monte Carlo qLogEI estimates the expectation from a fixed set of QMC samples. When the improvement region lies far in the tail of every candidate's distribution, no sample reaches it, and the estimator flattens near −51 for every batch. The greedy step then has nothing to compare. The optimizer therefore scores with

V(B) = max( MC qLogEI(B), maxi LogEI(xi) )

The second term is exact and cheap, and since a batch can never be worse than its best single member, it is a valid lower bound on log qEI. Where MC resolves the tail, the two agree. Where it cannot, the analytic term keeps the ranking informative. In the fantasized stage 1 the score is the weighted sum of V over the tree models, with the weights of section 2. In the example of Part III, MC resolves the tail at every step: V and the plain MC value differ by at most 0.003.

This GP almost interpolates the data. Far from the data it falls back to its prior: a random pool row sits at about 9 percent (2–31). 8. Refinement lifts the best single row from 64 to 71 percent

Of the 32 rows sent to refinement, 25 were local rows and 7 were random. The median single-row LogEI of the 32 rose from −3.169 to −2.265, and the best from −1.754 to −1.497. The best row moved only slightly, but into the peak: catalyst 1 in THF at 72.5 °C, 3.00 eq, loading 7.76 (median 64.3 percent, 43.7–81.3) became 73.3 °C, 2.91 eq, loading 8.30 (71.4 percent, 57.0–82.9). The next three local rows on catalyst 1 in THF started at different settings and converged to the same point. The refined block therefore contains duplicates, which costs nothing because greedy never picks a row that adds no value.

Step 2 is where greedy differs from ranking single rows. The rows with the best single values after step 1 are near-copies of the step-1 row, catalyst 1 in THF near 73 °C. As partners they add almost nothing: an exact duplicate leaves V at −1.497, because a second vial of the same experiment cannot beat the first. Step 2 asks which row adds the most expected improvement on top of step 1. The answer is catalyst 4 in DCM (−1.228). Its median is lower than that of the step-1 row (63.8 against 71.4 percent), but its band is the widest among the leading candidates, 43 to 81 percent, and its posterior correlation with the step-1 row is 0.52, so it has a fair chance of exceeding 67 percent in the outcomes where the step-1 row does not. The runner-up is catalyst 1 in THF at 76.7 °C (−1.276): less correlated with step 1 (0.13) but narrower (54 to 77 percent), so its chance of beating 67 percent is smaller. It enters the batch at step 3. Steps 4 to 6 add a new solvent each: ethyl acetate, Me-THF and HFIP. The step-6 row has the lowest median of the batch, 44 percent, but its band reaches 70 percent.

The temperature decisions follow the values. In step 2 the chosen row scores −1.228 at its own 72.4 °C and −1.234 when forced to 73.3 °C, so it opens group B. Step 3 opens the last group at 76.7 °C. From step 4 on no group can be opened, and the three remaining rows are placed by joining, one in each group, which completes (2, 2, 2).

10. Swaps and polish add 0.03

The swap pass replaced two of the six slots. Slot 1 (catalyst 1, THF, 8.30 loading) became the same catalyst and solvent at 2.89 eq and 7.74 loading. Slot 5 (catalyst 1, Me-THF) became catalyst 1 in THF at 3.00 eq and 9.20 loading. Together they raised V from −0.847 to −0.833. The polish moved 15 continuous variables (the six rows' formaldehyde and loading, plus the three group temperatures) for 32 L-BFGS-B iterations and raised V to −0.819. The group temperatures moved from 73.3, 72.4 and 76.7 °C to 73.0, 72.9 and 77.0 °C.

Groups A and B end 0.1 °C apart. The optimizer enforces that the batch uses at most three temperature values with two rows each. It does not enforce that the three values differ, so in the lab this batch runs at effectively two temperatures, four vials near 73 °C and two at 77 °C. If distinct heating blocks are required, the optimizer needs a minimum-separation constraint on the group temperatures.

Compared on 81 tree nodes from Ackley, Gallagher101 and LunacekBiRastrigin trajectories, with three seeds each, pool-greedy trails the GA by a median 0.04 nats at n ≈ 52. That is about 4 percent in EI. It leads by 0.50 nats at n ≈ 250 and by 17.3 nats at n ≈ 550, where the GA optimises into Monte Carlo noise. It is about 20 times faster per call (0.4–0.9 s against 9–11 s). The guarantee behind greedy is that expected improvement over a fixed pool has diminishing returns, and for such functions one-at-a-time greedy reaches at least 1 − 1/e ≈ 63 percent of the best subset of that pool. This guarantee does not carry over exactly here, because the optimizer maximises the log, the temperature overwrite changes the pool during the loop, and the pool is itself a sample of the space. The comparison above is the evidence that it holds in practice.
